# Dungeon Core — Genre Analysis

## What the genre is

**Dungeon Core** is a subgenre of LitRPG and progression fantasy. The protagonist is the dungeon itself—or more exactly the crystal or heart that powers it.

Representative works include Dakota Krout’s *Divine Dungeon* series (*Dungeon Born*), *There Is No Epic Loot Here, Only Puns*, and *Dungeon Core Online*, along with many Royal Road serials. Roots are in games like *Dungeon Keeper* and *War for the Overworld*, with the perspective flipped from the hero to the thing heroes invade.

## Core conventions

### The immobile protagonist

The core can’t move. Everything it does happens through the dungeon: rooms, tunnels, creatures, and traps. Its central weakness is that if anyone reaches and breaks the crystal, it dies. This gives the genre its tension: a powerful being with one fragile point.

### The origin hook

The protagonist is usually a reincarnated human, or a newly awakened core that slowly learns about the world. A typical early beat is waking up confused, with no body, and discovering what it can do.

### The guide companion

Most stories pair the core with a talkative helper—fairy, wisp, or bound spirit—who explains the rules, adds banter, and can act in places the core can’t reach. The companion carries much of the exposition and emotional connection.

### The essence economy

This is the genre’s engine. The core grows by absorbing mana or essence, mostly from things that die inside it: adventurers, monsters, plants, even dirt and ore. Absorbing a material or creature usually unlocks the ability to create it, building a **pattern library**.

### Territory and influence

The dungeon controls a radius that expands as it grows. Inside that area it can dig, shape, see, and spawn. Expanding territory is a major progression marker.

### Building and design

Readers come for base-building: designing floors, tunnels, chokepoints, trap gauntlets, monster spawners, puzzle rooms, and boss chambers. Clever design that rewards planning is the payoff.

### The adventurer paradox

The dungeon needs adventurers to enter, because their deaths and spent mana feed it. If it’s too deadly, people stop coming or send an army to destroy it. If it’s too easy, it starves. The dungeon becomes a careful balancer—loot as bait, difficulty tuned like a game designer.

### The world reacts

Towns, guilds, and economies grow up around a successful dungeon. Adventurer parties get names, rivalries, and story arcs. Factions emerge: some want to farm the dungeon, some want to tame it, some want to destroy it.

### Escalating threats

High-level hero parties, rival dungeons competing for territory, monster surges from deeper regions, and divine or political forces that treat dungeons as tools or abominations.

### Visible systems

Status screens, dungeon ranks or tiers, monster evolution trees, and mana or affinity types are common. Numbers going up is part of the appeal.

## Tone and themes

Tone ranges from lighthearted and pun-heavy to darker, morally gray stories.

Common themes:

- An alien mind slowly gaining (or losing) humanity
- The ethics of feeding on people
- The creator’s pride in its monsters
- The gap between how the dungeon sees itself and how the surface world sees it
- Named bosses and evolved minions with personality

## Implications for this game

| Pillar | Direction |
| --- | --- |
| **Core loop** | Gather essence → expand territory → build rooms and traps → spawn monsters → lure adventurers → absorb the dead → unlock new patterns → repeat |
| **Balance** | Manage reputation or danger value that controls who shows up; pushing difficulty too high draws retaliation |
| **Unlocks** | New monsters and traps from what the player **absorbs**, not only from leveling |
| **Companion** | Tutorial delivery and story |
| **Surface layer** | Nearby town grows or shrinks with dungeon reputation; named adventurers who return |
| **Stakes** | Core room defense is always the final line—one fragile point |
